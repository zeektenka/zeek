In 2008, a loaf of bread in Zimbabwe cost millions of dollars in the morning, and more by the afternoon. This is how a country's money stopped meaning anything.

To understand how strange that is, you have to start somewhere unexpected. Zimbabwe used to work.

In the 1980s and 90s this was a country that exported food. Tobacco, maize, beef. It had factories, a functioning banking system, and one of the best-educated populations on the continent. People called it the breadbasket of the region. Historians now argue that title was always a little generous, but it captured something real. Zimbabwe fed itself, and sold what was left over.

Then, across about a decade, that economy ran into three bills it could not pay.

The first arrived in 1997.

For years, veterans of Zimbabwe's liberation war had been protesting, demanding the pensions they had been promised. In November, President Robert Mugabe gave in. Fifty thousand veterans would each receive fifty thousand Zimbabwe dollars, plus monthly pensions.

None of it was in the budget. The total came to around four point two billion Zimbabwe dollars. About three percent of the entire economy, conjured out of nothing.

The markets understood immediately. On the fourteenth of November 1997, the Zimbabwe dollar fell by roughly seventy-two percent against the U.S. dollar. The stock market lost nearly half its value in a single day. Zimbabweans still call it Black Friday.

The second bill came a year later, when Zimbabwe sent troops into the war in the Democratic Republic of Congo. An expensive foreign war, for a country already short of cash.

The third was the biggest. From 2000, the government began seizing large commercial farms, mostly white-owned, and redistributing them. The politics of that are genuinely contested. This was land taken during colonial rule, and the argument for returning it was real. But the execution destroyed the thing that paid Zimbabwe's bills. Farms changed hands without equipment, without credit, without expertise. Output fell. Exports fell with it.

And exports were how Zimbabwe earned foreign currency.

So now the government had less coming in, far more going out, and nobody willing to lend to it.
