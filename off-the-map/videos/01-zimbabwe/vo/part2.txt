There was one option left.

A government that cannot tax enough, and cannot borrow enough, has a last resort. It can have its central bank create money, and lend it to the state. On paper, the bills get paid. The soldiers get paid. The civil servants get paid.

Under Gideon Gono, who ran the Reserve Bank of Zimbabwe from 2003, that is exactly what happened. At scale.

Here is the problem. Printing money does not create bread, or fuel, or electricity. It only creates claims on them. If the number of claims doubles while the amount of bread stays the same, the price of bread doubles. Nothing has been produced. The money has simply been diluted.

And once people notice it happening, something worse begins. They stop holding the currency. They spend it the moment they are paid, because tomorrow it buys less. That spending pushes prices up faster, which makes everybody spend faster still.

The currency does not just lose value. It loses value at an accelerating rate.

That loop is what hyperinflation actually is. Not high prices. A collapse of belief.

By July 2008, official monthly inflation was two thousand six hundred percent. Then the government simply stopped publishing the figures.

Independent economists kept measuring. Steve Hanke, of Johns Hopkins, estimated that by the middle of November 2008, inflation had reached seventy-nine point six billion percent a month. Annualised, that is eighty-nine point seven sextillion percent. An eight, a nine, and twenty-one more digits.

The number is so large it stops meaning anything. So here is what it meant on the ground.

Prices were roughly doubling every day. Wages were worthless before they could be spent. Shops repriced goods while customers were still queuing. People carried money in bags, and the bags were worth more than the notes.

In January 2009, the Reserve Bank issued a one hundred trillion dollar note. On the day it appeared, it was worth about thirty U.S. dollars.
