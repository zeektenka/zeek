What ended it was not a clever policy. It was surrender.

In early 2009, Zimbabwe gave up on its own currency and let people use U.S. dollars and South African rand instead. Inflation stopped almost immediately, because the thing causing it had been switched off.

That worked. But it cost Zimbabwe something most countries never surrender: control of its own money.

And the story did not end there. Bond notes in 2016. A new Zimbabwe dollar in 2019. And in April 2024, the Zig, Zimbabwe Gold, a currency the central bank promised would be backed by gold and hard currency reserves, precisely because nobody would trust a promise without them.

Today, more than eighty percent of transactions inside Zimbabwe still happen in foreign currency. Fifteen years on, the trust has not come back. That is the real cost. Not the funny banknote. The fact that a country can lose the ability to be believed, and may not get it back in a generation.

It is tempting to file this under things that happen to other people. It should not be.

Every government on earth can create money. The United States created trillions during the pandemic. So did the European Central Bank, the Bank of England, the Bank of Japan. And their currencies did not collapse. The dollar actually got stronger.

So what separates them? Why can some countries print money and get away with it, while Zimbabwe printed and died?

That is not a question about printing presses. It is a question about who trusts you, what you sell to the world, and who else needs your money.

And the answer decides what your savings are worth, wherever you keep them.
