# Video 1 — How Zimbabwe Printed Its Way to Sextillion Percent Inflation

## PART 1 (VO part 1 — 2,480 chars)

**[HOOK — 0:00]**

In 2008, a loaf of bread in Zimbabwe cost millions of dollars in the morning, and more by the afternoon. This is how a country's money stopped meaning anything.

**[CH 1 — THE BREADBASKET]**

To understand how strange that is, you have to start somewhere unexpected. Zimbabwe used to work.

In the 1980s and 90s this was a country that exported food. Tobacco, maize, beef. It had factories, a functioning banking system, and one of the best-educated populations on the continent. People called it the breadbasket of the region. Historians now argue that title was always a little generous, but it captured something real. Zimbabwe fed itself, and sold what was left over.

Then, across about a decade, that economy ran into three bills it could not pay.

**[CH 2 — THREE BILLS]**

The first arrived in 1997.

For years, veterans of Zimbabwe's liberation war had been protesting, demanding the pensions they had been promised. In November, President Robert Mugabe gave in. Fifty thousand veterans would each receive fifty thousand Zimbabwe dollars, plus monthly pensions.

None of it was in the budget. The total came to around four point two billion Zimbabwe dollars. About three percent of the entire economy, conjured out of nothing.

The markets understood immediately. On the fourteenth of November 1997, the Zimbabwe dollar fell by roughly seventy-two percent against the US dollar. The stock market lost nearly half its value in a single day. Zimbabweans still call it Black Friday.

The second bill came a year later, when Zimbabwe sent troops into the war in the Democratic Republic of Congo. An expensive foreign war, for a country already short of cash.

The third was the biggest. From 2000, the government began seizing large commercial farms, mostly white-owned, and redistributing them. The politics of that are genuinely contested. This was land taken during colonial rule, and the argument for returning it was real. But the execution destroyed the thing that paid Zimbabwe's bills. Farms changed hands without equipment, without credit, without expertise. Output fell. Exports fell with it.

And exports were how Zimbabwe earned foreign currency.

So now the government had less coming in, far more going out, and nobody willing to lend to it.

## PART 2 (VO part 2 — 2,600 chars)

**[CH 3 — THE PRINTING PRESS]**

There was one option left.

A government that cannot tax enough, and cannot borrow enough, has a last resort. It can have its central bank create money, and lend it to the state. On paper, the bills get paid. The soldiers get paid. The civil servants get paid.

Under Gideon Gono, who ran the Reserve Bank of Zimbabwe from 2003, that is exactly what happened. At scale.

Here is the problem. Printing money does not create bread, or fuel, or electricity. It only creates claims on them. If the number of claims doubles while the amount of bread stays the same, the price of bread doubles. Nothing has been produced. The money has simply been diluted.

And once people notice it happening, something worse begins. They stop holding the currency. They spend it the moment they are paid, because tomorrow it buys less. That spending pushes prices up faster, which makes everybody spend faster still.

The currency does not just lose value. It loses value at an accelerating rate.

That loop is what hyperinflation actually is. Not high prices. A collapse of belief.

**[CH 4 — THE NUMBERS]**

By July 2008, official monthly inflation was two thousand six hundred percent. Then the government simply stopped publishing the figures.

Independent economists kept measuring. Steve Hanke, of Johns Hopkins, estimated that by the middle of November 2008, inflation had reached seventy-nine point six billion percent a month. Annualised, that is eighty-nine point seven sextillion percent. An eight, a nine, and twenty-one more digits.

The number is so large it stops meaning anything. So here is what it meant on the ground.

Prices were roughly doubling every day. Wages were worthless before they could be spent. Shops repriced goods while customers were still queuing. People carried money in bags, and the bags were worth more than the notes.

In January 2009, the Reserve Bank issued a one hundred trillion dollar note. On the day it appeared, it was worth about thirty US dollars.

## PART 3 (VO part 3 — 2,180 chars)

**[CH 5 — THE AFTERMATH]**

What ended it was not a clever policy. It was surrender.

In early 2009, Zimbabwe gave up on its own currency and let people use US dollars and South African rand instead. Inflation stopped almost immediately, because the thing causing it had been switched off.

That worked. But it cost Zimbabwe something most countries never surrender: control of its own money.

And the story did not end there. Bond notes in 2016. A new Zimbabwe dollar in 2019. And in April 2024, the ZiG — Zimbabwe Gold — a currency the central bank promised would be backed by gold and hard currency reserves, precisely because nobody would trust a promise without them.

Today, more than eighty percent of transactions inside Zimbabwe still happen in foreign currency. Fifteen years on, the trust has not come back. That is the real cost. Not the funny banknote. The fact that a country can lose the ability to be believed, and may not get it back in a generation.

**[CLOSE — GLOBAL ANGLE]**

It is tempting to file this under things that happen to other people. It should not be.

Every government on earth can create money. The United States created trillions during the pandemic. So did the European Central Bank, the Bank of England, the Bank of Japan. And their currencies did not collapse. The dollar actually got stronger.

So what separates them? Why can some countries print money and get away with it, while Zimbabwe printed and died?

That is not a question about printing presses. It is a question about who trusts you, what you sell to the world, and who else needs your money.

And the answer decides what your savings are worth, wherever you keep them.

That is the next video.
