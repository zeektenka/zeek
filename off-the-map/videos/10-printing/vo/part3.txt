Now, the uncomfortable part. None of this makes printing free, even for America.

In June 2022, U.S. inflation hit nine point one percent. The highest in forty years. Groceries rose more than twelve percent over the year. Mortgages, rents, and borrowing costs followed, and the Federal Reserve spent the next two years raising interest rates to pull it back down.

That is what overdoing it looks like in a country the world trusts. Painful, expensive, politically brutal, but survivable. The currency bent. It did not break.

And the privilege is not permanent. At the start of this century, the dollar made up about seventy percent of global reserves. Today it is nearer fifty-seven. That decline is slow, and the dollar has no serious rival yet. But the direction is real.

That exorbitant privilege is not a law of physics. It is a reputation. Reputations can be spent.

So here is the practical version, wherever you live.

If you hold a currency the world wants, inflation is a tax you pay slowly. Painful over a decade. Survivable.

If you hold a currency the world does not want, your savings can disappear inside a single year. That is why, across much of Africa, Latin America and South Asia, people convert their savings into dollars the moment they get them. It is not a lack of patriotism. It is a rational response to having watched this happen before.

And it works the other way too. When the dollar strengthens, every country that borrowed in dollars, or imports food and fuel in dollars, suddenly pays more. A decision made in Washington changes the price of bread in Lagos, Cairo and Colombo.

The printing press is not the thing that matters. What matters is the queue of people who want what comes out of it.

Zimbabwe had the press. It never had the queue.
