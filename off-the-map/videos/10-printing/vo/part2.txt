Demand for the currency is half of it. The other half is having somewhere safe to park it.

If you are a country sitting on a large pile of dollars, you do not leave them in a vault. You buy U.S. government bonds. They pay interest, and they are the most easily sold financial asset on the planet.

Foreign investors and governments hold roughly thirty percent of U.S. government debt held by the public. Japan is the largest single foreign holder. China has been steadily reducing its pile, from a peak of about one point three trillion dollars in 2013, to something closer to seven hundred billion today.

Notice what that means. When the U.S. government borrows, there is a global queue of buyers waiting. When the Federal Reserve creates new dollars, a great deal of that money is absorbed by the rest of the world. Into reserves, into bonds, into assets. It does not all land on American shop shelves.

Zimbabwe had no queue. Its new money had nowhere to go but straight into domestic prices.

There is a second difference, and it matters just as much.

Inflation is not really about the amount of money. It is about money measured against the amount of stuff.

Through the pandemic, the American economy kept producing. Factories ran, farms ran, goods kept arriving. More money chased more or less the same quantity of goods, and prices rose uncomfortably.

In Zimbabwe, money was being created while production was falling off a cliff. Farms had stopped producing. Exports had collapsed. Factories could not get the foreign currency to buy inputs.

So it was not simply more money chasing the same goods. It was vastly more money chasing fewer goods, every month, and everyone could see it happening.

That is the accelerant. Once people are certain their money will be worth less tomorrow, they stop holding it at all. And a currency nobody will hold overnight is already dead. It just has not been announced yet.
