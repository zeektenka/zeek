# Video 10 — Why the US Can Print Money and Zimbabwe Can't

Target: ~8 min · Narration ~1,100 words at 2.3 words/sec
Angle: the companion to Video 1. Pulls high-value US/UK viewers, then sends them back to the Zimbabwe video. The real subject is *demand for a currency*, not printing.

---

## PART 1 (VO part 1 — 2,520 chars)

**[HOOK — 0:00]**

America printed trillions of dollars, and the dollar got stronger. Zimbabwe printed money, and its currency died. Same action. Opposite results.

Here is what actually separates them.

**[CH 1 — WHAT PRINTING ACTUALLY MEANS]**

First, let's be precise, because "printing money" is doing a lot of work in that sentence.

When a wealthy country creates money, it is almost never physical notes. A central bank creates reserves electronically, and uses them to buy assets. Mostly government bonds.

Between 2020 and 2022, the US Federal Reserve did this on an extraordinary scale. Its balance sheet roughly doubled, to around nine trillion dollars. New money, created on a keyboard, used to buy up government debt.

Strip away the technology, and the mechanism is not that different from what the Reserve Bank of Zimbabwe did. A central bank creates money. The government gets to spend.

So if the mechanism is similar, the outcome should be similar. It wasn't. Zimbabwe's currency went to zero. The dollar, over that period, went up.

The difference is not the printer. It is what sits on the other side of it.

**[CH 2 — THE QUEUE]**

Here is the thing the United States has that Zimbabwe never did: somebody else wants its money.

About fifty-seven percent of all the foreign exchange reserves held by the world's central banks are held in dollars. Not because anyone passed a law. Because the dollar is what the world settles up in.

Oil is priced in dollars. So are most metals, most shipping, most commodity contracts. A company in Nigeria buying machinery from Vietnam may well pay for it in dollars, even though no American is involved in the transaction.

That means every country on earth needs a stock of dollars, permanently, just to trade. Central banks hold them. Exporters hold them. Ordinary people in unstable economies hold them under the mattress.

That is a standing queue of demand for dollars, coming from outside America, that never really goes away.

Now ask the same question about the Zimbabwe dollar in 2008. Who, outside Zimbabwe, wanted one? Nobody. Not a single central bank was holding Zimbabwe dollars as a reserve. No commodity was priced in them.

## PART 2 (VO part 2 — 2,640 chars)

**[CH 3 — AND SOMEWHERE TO PUT THEM]**

Demand for the currency is half of it. The other half is having somewhere safe to park it.

If you are a country sitting on a large pile of dollars, you do not leave them in a vault. You buy US government bonds. They pay interest, and they are the most easily sold financial asset on the planet.

Foreign investors and governments hold roughly thirty percent of US government debt held by the public. Japan is the largest single foreign holder. China has been steadily reducing its pile, from a peak of about one point three trillion dollars in 2013 to something closer to seven hundred billion today.

Notice what that means. When the US government borrows, there is a global queue of buyers waiting. When the Federal Reserve creates new dollars, a great deal of that money is absorbed by the rest of the world: into reserves, into bonds, into assets. It does not all land on American shop shelves.

Zimbabwe had no queue. Its new money had nowhere to go but straight into domestic prices.

**[CH 4 — AND SOMETHING TO BUY]**

There is a second difference, and it matters just as much.

Inflation is not really about the amount of money. It is about money measured against the amount of stuff.

Through the pandemic, the American economy kept producing. Factories ran, farms ran, goods kept arriving. More money chased more or less the same quantity of goods, and prices rose uncomfortably.

In Zimbabwe, money was being created while production was falling off a cliff. Farms had stopped producing. Exports had collapsed. Factories could not get the foreign currency to buy inputs.

So it was not simply more money chasing the same goods. It was vastly more money chasing fewer goods, every month, and everyone could see it happening.

That is the accelerant. Once people are certain their money will be worth less tomorrow, they stop holding it at all. And a currency nobody will hold overnight is already dead. It just has not been announced yet.

## PART 3 (VO part 3 — 2,420 chars)

**[CH 5 — THE LIMITS]**

Now, the uncomfortable part. None of this makes printing free, even for America.

In June 2022, US inflation hit nine point one percent. The highest in forty years. Groceries rose more than twelve percent over the year. Mortgages, rents, and borrowing costs followed, and the Federal Reserve spent the next two years raising interest rates to pull it back down.

That is what overdoing it looks like in a country the world trusts. Painful, expensive, politically brutal — but survivable. The currency bent. It did not break.

And the privilege is not permanent. At the start of this century, the dollar made up about seventy percent of global reserves. Today it is nearer fifty-seven. That decline is slow, and the dollar has no serious rival yet. But the direction is real.

That exorbitant privilege is not a law of physics. It is a reputation. Reputations can be spent.

**[CH 6 — WHAT IT MEANS FOR YOU]**

So here is the practical version, wherever you live.

If you hold a currency the world wants, inflation is a tax you pay slowly. Painful over a decade. Survivable.

If you hold a currency the world does not want, your savings can disappear inside a single year. That is why, across much of Africa, Latin America and South Asia, people convert their savings into dollars the moment they get them. It is not a lack of patriotism. It is a rational response to having watched this happen before.

And it works the other way too. When the dollar strengthens, every country that borrowed in dollars or imports food and fuel in dollars suddenly pays more. A decision made in Washington changes the price of bread in Lagos, Cairo and Colombo.

The printing press is not the thing that matters. What matters is the queue of people who want what comes out of it.

Zimbabwe had the press. It never had the queue.

If you want to see what that looks like from the inside, the full story of how Zimbabwe got there is the video on screen now.
